I’ll focus on a few examples from IWAC: preparing sources, building research tools, and deciding what to read. We can go into more detail in the discussion.

It started with a practical problem. The Islam West Africa Collection, or IWAC for short, is an online database I built, and it had grown well beyond what I could read on my own.

I work in two connected roles: historian of Islam and Muslim public life in francophone West Africa, and data curator helping other researchers use those sources.

Building it has made those two roles hard to separate. Decisions about transcription, names and interfaces shape the histories we can write. So: what does AI change in that work, and who has the resources to benefit?

0:50–2:50 · 120 seconds

The IWAC grew out of fieldwork. Since 2011 I have worked in Benin, Togo, Côte d’Ivoire and Burkina Faso, and I spent countless hours scanning press clippings and Islamic publications, and saving material published online — including nearly 574 hours of YouTube video, just to watch once. That became a large personal digital collection. I wanted others to be able to use it, so I built a public database now covering six West African countries. Making it public also meant clearing rights, so I reached out to editors for permission to share their material.

I’m a digital hoarder: I scan everything I can and scrape websites. My Zotero library alone holds 38,142 records, and about twenty thousand IWAC items came from it.

The work was artisanal. I’m not an archivist, and I have no team: I catalogued everything myself — the metadata, the people named, the places, the organisations. Early on that was manageable. As the collection grew, I could no longer keep up on my own, and that is where AI became interesting.

My case may be extreme, but I suspect the experience is increasingly common among historians. Think of the “scanning parties” in archives: we come home with thousands of disorganised photographs, intending to read them later. The time to consult them never seems to come. We can accumulate sources much faster than we can examine them.

And the pile itself has a shape. Mine is predominantly French, reflecting where I worked and what could be shared. By the time AI arrives, the collection already carries those choices.

2:50–3:45 · 55 seconds

In this workflow, multimodal models produce draft transcriptions from scanned pages. I also use language models to identify people, places and organisations. This makes more of the collection searchable and available for computational analysis.

The output still needs checking. A language model may produce fluent text while misreading a name, changing a spelling or filling in an illegible passage. These errors can be difficult to notice because the transcription remains readable.

The same problem occurs in indexing. A name match can merge two different people, while an omitted place or organisation may make relevant documents harder to find.

I also use AI to generate code for the collection’s interfaces. I describe what I need, test the result and ask for revisions. This has made it easier for me to develop tools around my own research questions.

The generated code still needs checking. A map may display correctly while counting mentions in a way I did not intend; a filter may exclude relevant documents. For some problems, I still need technical help.

What should I read next?

AI-assisted sentiment analysis · 12,349 newspaper articles

iwac.frederickmadore.com

6:55–8:20 · 85 seconds

So far, I’ve shown how I use AI to transcribe sources, extract information and build tools. I’ve also experimented with taking it a step further: a first pass at analysis.

I asked five AI models to analyse over twelve thousand newspaper articles along three dimensions: polarity—whether Islam and Muslims are portrayed favourably, unfavourably or neutrally; centrality—how prominently they feature; and subjectivity—how much opinion or judgement is expressed about them.

This dashboard lets me explore those assessments, compare the models and go back to the articles. For example, I can look for contrasting portrayals or articles where the models disagree.

I use these scores to select articles for closer reading. But a negative score needs interpretation: is the article criticising Muslims, quoting someone who does, or reporting discrimination against them? Comparing models helps me identify cases worth checking, although agreement between models is not enough to validate a classification. I need to return to the article to understand what the score captures and what it leaves out.

8:20–9:30 · 70 seconds

These experiments also raise a practical question: what resources make them possible?

My sentiment-analysis experiment cost about ten dollars per model for processing. That figure does not include the work of preparing the corpus or assessing the results. Commercial services limit what I need to run on my own hardware, but they leave me dependent on an external provider.

Locally run open-weight models offer more control, with hardware requirements that vary by model. At Bayreuth, I have access to powerful servers. I cannot assume that colleagues elsewhere, including at African universities, have access to comparable resources.

For IWAC, I am processing publications intended for public circulation, not confidential research records. This is relevant to my choice of tools, but it does not settle every question about reuse or provider dependence. I use commercial services while also exploring locally run alternatives.

9:30–10:45 · 75 seconds

But access to AI models is only part of the story. Which sources actually benefit?

For printed sources, that starts with preservation and digitisation. AI can help process a scan; it doesn’t replace the tedious work of keeping newspapers together or photographing them page by page.

Most of my workflows also concern French. The Ewé example is promising, and my tests with Hausa recordings are very encouraging. But Hausa is a major African language; I’d expect less reliable results for languages with fewer digital resources.

That work still needs archivists, librarians and people with the linguistic and historical knowledge to assess what the models produce.

So for me, democratisation isn’t just about making AI available. It also means supporting the collections and the people who make these sources usable. Otherwise, we risk making already digitised collections more visible while other sources remain out of reach.

AI has helped me make more sources accessible, build my own tools and find starting points for close reading.

That expands what I can do as a historian. But historical judgement belongs throughout the process—not just at the end.

For me, this opens possibilities for democratisation. Yet wider participation still depends on support for collections, people and infrastructure.

The goal isn’t simply to process more sources, but to help more people work with them.
