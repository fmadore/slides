Thank you, James, for the kind invitation to join this panel.

In about ten minutes I’ll give you the broad picture: how AI arrived in my work, and what I’m experimenting with. We can go into detail in the discussion.

It started with a practical problem. The Islam West Africa Collection, or IWAC for short, is an online database I built, and it had grown well beyond what I could read on my own.

I work in two connected roles: historian of Islam and Muslim public life in francophone West Africa, and data curator helping other researchers use those sources.

Building it has made those two roles hard to separate. Decisions about transcription, names and interfaces shape the histories we can write. So: what does AI change in that work, and who has the resources to benefit?

0:50–2:50 · 120 seconds

The IWAC grew out of fieldwork. Since 2011 I have worked in Benin, Togo, Côte d’Ivoire and Burkina Faso, and I spent countless hours scanning press clippings and Islamic publications, and saving material published online — including nearly 574 hours of YouTube video, just to watch once. That became a large personal digital collection. I wanted others to be able to use it, so I built a public database now covering six West African countries. Making it public also meant clearing rights, so I reached out to editors for permission to share their material.

I’m a digital hoarder: I scan everything I can and scrape websites. My Zotero library alone holds 38,142 records, and about twenty thousand IWAC items came from it.

The work was artisanal. I’m not an archivist, and I have no team: I catalogued everything myself — the metadata, the people named, the places, the organisations. Early on that was manageable. As the collection grew, I could no longer keep up on my own, and that is where AI became interesting.

My case may be extreme, but I suspect the experience is increasingly common among historians. Think of the “scanning parties” in archives: we come home with thousands of disorganised photographs, intending to read them later. The time to consult them never seems to come. We can accumulate sources much faster than we can examine them.

And the pile itself has a shape. Mine is predominantly French, reflecting where I worked and what could be shared. By the time AI arrives, the collection already carries those choices.

2:50–4:15 · 85 seconds

In the last two or three years, multimodal AI models have become very good at reading documents — printed text, and even handwriting, at a level traditional OCR and HTR never reached. Small models can now pull out named entities, people, places and organisations, well enough to catalogue a collection.

For a historian moving towards computational methods, that is the first step: sources have to be machine-readable. Corpora that were unusable for lack of OCR are now workable.

Here is the catch. When traditional OCR fails, you see it: the page comes out as garbage. When a language model fails, the output stays plausible. It may quietly correct a spelling that was wrong in the original. If a word is illegible, it may infer one, or invent one. The transcription reads well, so you conclude it is right.

The same applies further down the chain: a name match can join two different people; an index can drop what another historian would have kept.

4:40–5:30 · 50 seconds

AI has also changed what I can build as a historian. Until recently, building an interactive map or network often meant finding a developer, a budget and institutional support.

Now, I describe what I need, test the result and ask for changes. The AI writes the code; I guide the process.

For me, that’s a form of democratisation: I can build tools around my own research questions without writing the code myself.

But a working interface isn’t necessarily a reliable one. I still need to check what it counts and what its filters exclude—and sometimes get technical help.

7:30–8:55 · 85 seconds

So far, I’ve shown how I use AI to transcribe sources, extract information and build tools. I’ve also experimented with taking it a step further: a first pass at analysis.

I asked five AI models to analyse over twelve thousand newspaper articles along three dimensions: polarity—whether Islam and Muslims are portrayed favourably, unfavourably or neutrally; centrality—how prominently they feature; and subjectivity—how much opinion or judgement is expressed about them.

This dashboard lets me explore those assessments, compare the models and go back to the articles. For example, I can look for contrasting portrayals or articles where the models disagree.

For me, this is a form of triage: I can’t read everything, so these provisional assessments help me decide where to start. They don’t replace close reading. Of course, using AI to guide our attention raises epistemological questions, which we can come back to in the discussion.

8:55–10:20 · 85 seconds

The same tension applies to the models themselves. In the experiment described in the article, a commercial run over the newspaper corpus cost roughly ten US dollars per model. That is the processing bill; it leaves out development and review.

Services at that price are attractive to a small project. Yet they tie the work to external providers, including companies based in the United States and China. Payment arrangements, connectivity, terms of service and changes to access all matter.

I have also begun running open-weight models on university computing infrastructure at Bayreuth. That gives me greater control over processing and the model version I retain.

But downloadable weights still require machines, electricity, expertise and institutional support. Using those weights through a commercial host preserves the hosting dependency.

The resources available to me cannot be assumed for an archive or colleague elsewhere. For African Studies, that makes the distribution of institutional capacity central to any claim about wider access.

10:20–11:35 · 75 seconds

I see three connected stakes for African Studies.

First, language. Most of the textual work I have described concerns French. Experiments with Hausa recordings broaden the agenda, but establish little about performance on languages such as Dendi or Ewe. Priorities in processing can reproduce existing hierarchies of visibility.

Second, labour. Faster processing of available scans leaves the work of preserving and digitising further holdings in place. Verification creates its own demands. Institutions need support for all of those tasks.

Third, authority over circulation. Open access creates opportunities for reuse, while also exposing material to automated harvesting. The IWAC has experienced this, although we do not know whether harvested material entered model training.

These questions connect AI to longstanding concerns in African digital history: who makes sources available, who can interpret them, and who sets the conditions under which knowledge circulates.

11:35–12:30 · 55 seconds

AI has helped me share and explore a collection that had exceeded my capacity to process it manually. That practical gain matters.

My experience also brings the historian and curator together. Deciding what a name means, how a text should be transcribed, or what a connection counts as is already part of historical work.

The responsibility is to make those choices visible enough for others to inspect, challenge and revise, while recognising the labour and unequal resources behind them.

For me, that is the useful question to bring to this panel: under what conditions can AI expand access to African historical sources while sustaining the expertise needed to interpret them?
