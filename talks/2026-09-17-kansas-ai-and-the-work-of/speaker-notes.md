## 1. AI and the work of the historian

0:00–0:50 · 50 seconds

Thank you, James, for the kind invitation to join this panel.

My own experience with AI began with a practical problem: I had assembled a collection of sources that had grown beyond what I could read and process on my own.

I work in two connected roles. As a historian, I study Islam and Muslim public life in francophone West Africa. As a data curator, I help other researchers find and use these sources.

Working on the Islam West Africa Collection has made those roles increasingly difficult to separate. Decisions about transcription, names and interfaces shape the histories we can write. I want to discuss what AI changes in that work, and who has the resources to benefit.

## 2. Abundance has a history

0:50–2:50 · 120 seconds

The IWAC grew out of fieldwork that began in 2011. I collected press clippings, Islamic publications and private holdings. Requests from colleagues helped turn that material into a public resource across six West African countries.

My case may be extreme. I’m a digital hoarder: I scan everything I can and scrape websites. My Zotero holdings add up to 38,142 records. About twenty thousand IWAC items came from Zotero, so the public collection already draws on those holdings, with much more potentially available to integrate.

[Zotero count for reference: 6,678 + 15,000 + 8,889 + 1,075 + 6,500 = 38,142. These holdings overlap with IWAC; do not add the two totals.]

But I suspect the experience is increasingly common among historians. Think of the “scanning parties” in archives: we come home with thousands of disorganised photographs, intending to read them later. The time to consult them never seems to come. We can accumulate sources much faster than we can examine them.

Here, that includes 34,468 minutes of YouTube videos, nearly 574 hours just to watch once. How do we process that? Transcription, time-coded indexing and checking passages against the recording could help us find material to study. The voice, images and context still need our attention.

This abundance has a history of labour by archivists, librarians, publishers, collectors and assistants. It also has gaps: the predominantly French corpus reflects where I worked, what survived and what could be shared. AI enters that already shaped collection.

## 3. Curation already interprets

2:50–4:00 · 70 seconds

AI helped me address a practical backlog: making scanned documents searchable and connecting references to the same people, places and organisations.

But these operations involve historical judgement. A transcription may quietly regularise an unusual spelling. A name match may join records that concern different people. An index may leave out an announcement that another historian would consider significant.

The difficulty is that the output can look convincing. A fluent sentence gives us fewer visible warning signs than garbled text. A clean record can conceal an uncertain identification.

So I keep the images available alongside the transcriptions, distinguish generated additions and return to the sources. The article describes practical experiments rather than a systematic accuracy benchmark.

My two roles meet here: the choices I make as a curator become part of the evidence that I, and others, encounter as historians.

## 4. Where does the press direct our attention?

4:00–5:20 · 80 seconds

This map is one way into the collection. The circles locate places mentioned in its records, and their size reflects how often those places occur. We can see the concentration in West Africa and references extending towards the Arabian Peninsula.

[Point to West Africa, then the Arabian Peninsula. Stay on the saved image.]

For a historian of Islam, that opens questions about pilgrimage, educational trajectories and diplomatic relationships. We can select an actor and ask which places appear in the documents associated with them, then read those documents.

The map also requires restraint. It counts documentary mentions. It cannot tell us how many Muslims lived in a place, or establish that a person travelled there. Large circles may reflect publication coverage, indexing or collecting priorities.

Its value is to help formulate a question and locate evidence. Explaining the geography still requires knowledge of the newspapers, their audiences and the circumstances of publication.

## 5. What does a connection mean?

5:20–6:40 · 80 seconds

A network offers a different way to navigate. Here, nodes represent indexed people, organisations, places, events and subjects. Connections record their appearance in the same items.

[Point to a cluster. The audience does not need to read every label.]

That can help us locate sources about Muslim associations and the political actors around them, or follow an organisation across different publications. We gain an entry point that a search for one exact spelling might miss.

But a co-mention leaves the nature of the connection open. An article might report cooperation, conflict, a quotation or a passing comparison. Establishing an alliance, a teaching relationship or a shared institution takes additional evidence.

The network also inherits earlier curatorial decisions. If two people have been merged under one name, their apparent connections merge too. I use the visualisation to move between a broader view and particular documents, with the historical meaning of a link still to be established.

## 6. What a label leaves out

6:40–8:05 · 85 seconds

One example from the article makes this movement concrete. I experimented with AI classifications of portrayals of Islam and Muslims in more than twelve thousand newspaper articles. The annotations are provisional ways of organising material for reading.

In coverage of police interventions at mosques in Abobo, published in La Voie in June 1994, most models assigned a favourable portrayal of Muslims. Another assigned a negative label even though its explanation described Muslims as innocent victims defended by the newspaper.

That disagreement helped bring the article into view. Yet resolving the label only gets us part of the way. Sympathy for worshippers also contributed to a denunciation of the police and the government.

As a historian, I want to understand how the newspaper uses that solidarity in a political argument. A positive score compresses that distinction. This example shows how an annotation can lead to a richer question. It does not establish the accuracy of classifications across the corpus or the prevalence of a portrayal in the national press.

## 7. More people can build. Who can verify?

8:05–9:15 · 70 seconds

I built these interfaces with coding assistance from language models. With limited formal training in computer science, I could describe what I wanted, ask for changes and develop tools suited to the collection.

That is a meaningful expansion of participation. Historians with little programming support can take a more direct role in designing how their sources are explored.

But a working chart can still count the wrong things. It may exclude undated records or compare raw totals from very unequal corpora. Those choices affect the argument even when the interface looks polished.

Verification therefore needs time, some code literacy and access to help when necessary. Asking the same agent whether its work is correct gives us limited reassurance.

I would describe this as a partial democratisation of tool building. The threshold for producing an interface has fallen, while the ability to assess and maintain it remains unevenly distributed.

## 8. Affordable access, unequal control

9:15–10:40 · 85 seconds

The same tension applies to the models themselves. In the experiment described in the article, a commercial run over the newspaper corpus cost roughly ten US dollars per model. That is the processing bill; it leaves out development and review.

Services at that price are attractive to a small project. Yet they tie the work to external providers, including companies based in the United States and China. Payment arrangements, connectivity, terms of service and changes to access all matter.

I have also begun running open-weight models on university computing infrastructure at Bayreuth. That gives me greater control over processing and the model version I retain.

But downloadable weights still require machines, electricity, expertise and institutional support. Using those weights through a commercial host preserves the hosting dependency.

The resources available to me cannot be assumed for an archive or colleague elsewhere. For African Studies, that makes the distribution of institutional capacity central to any claim about wider access.

## 9. Whose sources become usable?

10:40–11:55 · 75 seconds

I see three connected stakes for African Studies.

First, language. Most of the textual work I have described concerns French. Experiments with Hausa recordings broaden the agenda, but establish little about performance on languages such as Dendi or Ewe. Priorities in processing can reproduce existing hierarchies of visibility.

Second, labour. Faster processing of available scans leaves the work of preserving and digitising further holdings in place. Verification creates its own demands. Institutions need support for all of those tasks.

Third, authority over circulation. Open access creates opportunities for reuse, while also exposing material to automated harvesting. The IWAC has experienced this, although we do not know whether harvested material entered model training.

These questions connect AI to longstanding concerns in African digital history: who makes sources available, who can interpret them, and who sets the conditions under which knowledge circulates.

## 10. Make the archive searchable. Keep its making visible.

11:55–12:50 · 55 seconds

AI has helped me share and explore a collection that had exceeded my capacity to process it manually. That practical gain matters.

My experience also brings the historian and curator together. Deciding what a name means, how a text should be transcribed, or what a connection counts as is already part of historical work.

The responsibility is to make those choices visible enough for others to inspect, challenge and revise, while recognising the labour and unequal resources behind them.

For me, that is the useful question to bring to this panel: under what conditions can AI expand access to African historical sources while sustaining the expertise needed to interpret them?
