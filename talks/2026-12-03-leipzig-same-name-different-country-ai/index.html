I'll start with one record. Until October, the person record Mamadou Bamba, item 1314, existed because of a scholarly reference: a researcher who wrote on the Sufi orders of Côte d'Ivoire in 2002.

Name matching had attached to it the press mentions of three other men: a politician, an army officer and an imam, two Ivorian and one Burkinabè, between 1986 and 2015. A reader opening the researcher's page found articles about a failed coup and a Ramadan prayer.

The record held one name and the type person. Nothing in it could tell these four apart.

For those who have not seen it, this is the Islam West Africa Collection, IWAC. It grew out of my fieldwork in Benin, Togo, Côte d'Ivoire and Burkina Faso, where I scanned press clippings and Islamic publications. I wanted other researchers to be able to use them, so I built a public database. It is open access and hosted at the Leibniz-Zentrum Moderner Orient in Berlin.

Most of the text comes from newspapers, predominantly in French, digitised and run through OCR. Islamic periodicals, documents, recordings, photographs and the scholarly literature sit alongside them.

I'm not an archivist, and I work on the collection mostly alone: the collecting, the cataloguing, the code and the AI pipelines. That is why I built the workflow in this talk: no single person can read what the collection now holds.

Names are unstable in the other direction too. Idriss Koudouss Koné, an Ivorian imam of the Conseil National Islamique, is in the press from 1992 to 2020, and his record carries nineteen alternative names. Newspapers reverse his names, add El Hadj or Imam, write Koudouss with one s, and call him Idriss, Idrissa or Drissa. Each of these strings is also a matching key.

One preferred label consolidates a historical figure; it also flattens the naming practices of the sources. My normalisation rules strip El Hadj from the preferred name. The rule buys consistency at the cost of the pilgrimage status the source thought worth recording.

What IWAC stores The link and the record. The mention is not kept as an object of its own.

A mention is what the source gives us: a string, in an article, at a date, in a context. A link is a claim, made by a person or a program, that this mention is that entity. And an authority record asserts four things at once: one entity, one preferred name, a type and a country.

This is where IWAC differs from a factoid model. The article points straight at the record, and a string such as "Imam Koudouss" is not stored as an object of its own. So a wrong link looks exactly like a right one, and unless something records which model or script made it, nobody can trace where it came from.

The workflow

Which steps are model calls, which are code, which are agents

Now the workflow, step by step, with who does what.

Five steps, and who acts at each

[on arrival: step 1] The workflow as run on the Burkinabè press in October. Step one: Claude Haiku 5.5 reads each article once and returns the persons, organisations, places and a few subjects. One model call per article, with no tools.

[→ step 2] Step two, matching, is code with no model: exact keys first, looser keys only when every exact key has missed. A name that fits several records, or only approximately, is left unlinked.

[→ step 3] Step three decides the country. The same model rereads each article with its candidates, and keeps, chooses or drops each link, only among the ids it was offered.

[→ step 4] Step four: scripts write after a dry run, and every link names the model that proposed it.

[→ step 5] Step five handles the names nothing matched. Here, and only here, I use agents in the strict sense: Claude Code subagents, one per batch, proposing new records, or another name for a record that already exists. I decide each proposal, and a script writes what I approve.

The loop at the bottom matters. An alternative name I accept becomes a matching key, so the next run links that form at step two, without a model.

How the country is decided

Rules in the prompt
  • “Use the text first, the newspaper’s country second.”
  • “A body of another country is not this one.”
  • A contradiction is only “a different country, an incompatible calling … or dates that cannot fit”.
  • “Choose only among the candidates of that entry. Never invent an id.”

Keep Choose Drop: leave unlinked

Illustration of the input’s shape, not the transcript of a real call

Descriptions decide A name cannot separate namesakes; a description gives country, calling and period. 1,021 of 1,611 were drafted by reviewer agents, then approved.

I wrote the rules into the prompt. The text comes first, the newspaper's country second. Only three things count as a contradiction: another country, an incompatible calling, or dates that cannot fit.

On the right, as an illustration, is the shape of what it receives for one name: Kassoum Coulibaly in a Burkinabè paper, with an Ivorian record cited in 1982 and 1983 and the Burkinabè defence minister as candidates. It can keep, choose or drop, and the code refuses any id it was not offered. Leaving a name unlinked is an acceptable answer.

What lets it choose is the description. Two records named Kassoum Coulibaly are identical by name; one line saying "minister of defence, Burkina Faso" is what the article can agree or disagree with. The three contradictions in the prompt, country, calling and dates, are exactly what a description supplies. So writing descriptions became part of the linking work: reviewer agents draft one or two sentences from the archive's own articles, I approve them in a plan, and each carries the model that wrote it, iwac:descriptionModel. Of the 1,611 records that have a description, 1,021 were drafted this way. 4,313 records still have none, and for those the check has only the name and the country.

The rules came from mistakes. After the pilot, the model dropped Souleymane Compaoré because his description says "former"; the prompt now says descriptions are written today. After the full run, it dropped Tertius Zongo for being described in another post. A new rule says a role the description does not mention is no contradiction, and on a re-check the drops fell from 2,656 to 1,776.

First, an acronym. Burkina Faso, Côte d’Ivoire and Benin each have a national committee for the pilgrimage to Mecca, with nearly identical names, and the press calls more than one of them CNOPM. A second Burkinabè CNOPM record, number 654, held 32 articles, most from Ivorian and Beninese newspapers.

On 21 October the model read each article and gave the passage that decided where it belonged. I read those passages and approved the moves: twelve to the Ivorian committee, two to a Burkinabè record, one to the AMOP. Ten I kept back, and I settled the record as a whole two days later.

The last seven came from Daho-Express and Ehuzu, between 1972 and 1983, and described a state committee chaired by the interior minister, then the foreign minister. IWAC had no record for it. The review recovered an entity the collection did not know it had; its record is on the right.

Since then, every generic national body carries its country in its title.

“Unknown” has to be representable

Authors and actors share one person set

Second, back to the record I started with. Mamadou Bamba, record 1314, is in IWAC because of a scholarly reference: a researcher who wrote in 2002 on the Sufi communities of Côte d’Ivoire. Authors and people in the press share one person set, so his record had collected the press mentions of three other men.

After reading the articles, I kept 1314 for the researcher and created three new records: the Ivorian politician, the Burkinabè lieutenant-colonel and the deputy imam of Yamoussoukro.

One mention fitted nowhere. A 1979 article names an “El Hadji Mamadou Bamba”, and nothing says which of these men he is, or whether he is a fifth. So the link was removed and no record created. In IWAC, removing the link is the only way to say “unknown”.

Likewise, Boukary Savadogo the scholar, record 1295, is not the ambassador in Riyadh, now record 127895.

Third, acronyms that are also ordinary words, which no rule had anticipated. Twelve of the 408 acronyms in the authority records are. “UN” appears almost 44,000 times in lower case: it is the French indefinite article.

The Front de la Oumma Islamique, an Ivorian organisation known as the FOI, had 410 links. The model reread them, a full-text check tested its verdicts, and 317 were the French word for faith, including every Burkinabè, Beninese and Togolese link. I approved their removal; four the model wanted to drop did name the Front, and stayed. In the Burkinabè run, the context check had already dropped 199 matches of “Foi”, and 27 of “Aide”.

The fix sits in the matching code: an acronym is now a key only when it is written in capitals.

The same thing happens with places, and with people. Danemark, record 428, had 124 links, and 93 of them were Togolese agency reports that never mention Denmark. They name Danyi and Dankpen, two prefectures of Togo. The model read all 124, its verdicts matched a plain word count exactly, and the 93 were removed.

On the right, the reverse: a man read as a country. Moustapha Diaby, who led the Conseil supérieur islamique of Côte d'Ivoire, was known in the press simply as "Koweït". Eighteen links on the record for Kuwait were his nickname, an Abidjan quarter of that name, or nothing at all. He also had two records, which I merged, and a namesake, a physician who died in 2020, had left his date of death on one of them.

I cannot tell you who made these errors. None of the Danemark links and seventeen of the eighteen carry no model annotation, so nothing records which pass, or which person, created them. The same review also found what was missing: 48 articles named Diaby without a link to him, and 219 mentioned Kuwait without a link to the country. A check that only removes links does not see those.

What stays human

One curator, some 50,000 links from one country run

That leaves the question of what stays with the human, when one person faces some fifty thousand links from a single country.

Where the curator sits

  1. Rules before runsWritten by the curator, rewritten after reading what went wrong
  2. Ambiguity is never settled by codeThe model chooses among the records offered, or leaves the name unlinked
  3. Every new record is approved731 proposed in the first pass: 18 held back as possibly existing, 39 flagged

Human in the loop does not mean here that a person checks every link. The Burkinabè run alone wrote about fifty thousand. So I sit at five fixed points.

I write the rules and rewrite them after reading what went wrong: “descriptions are written today” came from a batch of wrong decisions I had read. Code never settles an ambiguity. New records reach the archive only through plans I have read: of 731 proposals in the first pass, 18 were held back as possibly existing, and 39 more had been flagged.

Merges and splits stay mine. Turned into pairs, the reviewers’ remarks would have folded the Yirgou clashes into the village, and the arrest of Inoussa Kanazoé into the man himself. I withdrew 59 such pairs. And no agent writes to the archive: scripts do, after a dry run, with a backup of every item.

So the human reviews decisions about records and the rules that produce links, not the links themselves.

What escapes the loop is what no rule anticipated.

There are three kinds of check here, and they are not worth the same.

The context check is a second reading, but by the same model that did the extraction, with the same blind spots. I would not call it independent.

The deterministic test looks for the name in the full text. It overturned four of the model’s drop verdicts on the FOI and ten on the other acronyms: articles that did name the organisation. On Denmark, the count and the model agreed on every link. A count is blunt, but it fails differently from the model.

The third would be a human sample, links drawn at random and checked by hand. It does not exist yet, so I have no error rate to give you.

No rule anticipated foi or Danyi, and that is how they got in.

Four modelling decisions the cases forced

Country in the title

For generic bodies, identity includes jurisdiction.

Unlinked as an outcome

A mention may stay a mention when the source cannot settle it.

Provenance on each link

iwac:nerModel, iwac:descriptionModel: which model proposed this value.

Undated descriptions

Written today about articles decades old. Patched by a prompt rule; IWAC lacks a temporal marker.

For generic bodies, identity includes jurisdiction, so the country now sits in the title. An unlinked mention counts as a result, as with the 1979 Mamadou Bamba. Provenance goes on each link, naming the model that proposed it; Denmark showed what happens without it.

The fourth is unresolved. Descriptions are written today, about articles decades old, and “ancien président” in one made the model drop a correct link. A prompt rule fixed it, but only as a patch: IWAC has no equivalent of your temporal marker, a date on every statement.

This is where, in my reading, the two projects meet. A factoid model keeps statements plural and tied to their sources, but each factoid still has to attach to some entity. The authority layer sits above the factoids, and that is where the singular, persisting group comes back in.

Questions for the Alevi Archive

  1. Where does the identity of a person or a cemaat live, and who decides that two factoids are about the same entity?
  2. Can a probabilistic identification stay marked as such once it is in the graph?
  3. Is a check by the same model a verification at all? What would count as an independent one?
  4. When the source cannot settle an identity: an unlinked mention, a provisional record, or a “possibly the same as” relation?

These four questions come out of the cases I have shown, and I suspect each has a counterpart in your sources, which also cross borders and empires. The first is the one I would most like to take up tomorrow morning, once we have seen your data model: where identity lives when every statement is plural. The last one I have answered for IWAC, provisionally, with the unlinked mention. I am not sure it is the right answer for a graph. Thank you, and I look forward to the discussion.

Appendix

Held back for questions

How step 2 matches names

Code, no model. Looser keys are tried only once every exact key has missed.

  • Exact keys: lower case, no accents, spaces or dashes
  • A key shared by two records is ambiguous, never resolved
  • Word order: “Coulibaly Kassoum” = “Kassoum Coulibaly”; never for initials
  • Plural fold for non-person subjects; accents kept, “Jeunes” ≠ “Jeûne”
  • Acronyms of 2–6 capitals get a case-sensitive key only
  • “(Country)” titles are also keyed bare within that country’s run

These are the rules of the deterministic matching step. Ambiguous and fuzzy terms are never linked by name alone; only the context check may link them, and only to a record it was offered.
